\begin{abstract}
DuskCrayfish learns what a small urban creek normally looks like and then flags
moments it cannot predict well. It takes a window of recent sensor history
across all the connected monitoring sites and predicts what the very next
reading should be at every site; when the real reading and the prediction
diverge sharply on conductivity, that gap is the anomaly signal. We never tell
the model what a spill looks like. It only ever learns normal, and an anomaly is
whatever it fails to reproduce. This document sets out the mathematics of the
deployed model, that is, a graph convolution over the creek's flow topology
followed by a per-node recurrent forecaster, together with the detection rules
we built on top of it and the reasoning behind each choice. We kept the
architecture small, and we give our reasons for this alongside the mathematics.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

DuskCrayfish learns what the creek normally looks like and then flags moments it
cannot predict well. Everything below follows from one idea: we take a
window of recent sensor history across all the connected sites, and we predict
what the very next reading should be at every site. When the real reading and
the prediction diverge sharply on conductivity, that gap is the anomaly signal.
We never tell the model what a spill looks like. It only ever learns normal, and
an anomaly is whatever it fails to reproduce.

This document explains the math and, alongside each piece, why we built the
model the way we did. The architecture is deliberately small, and we explain
what led us to that at each point where the choice arises.

One graph layer, one LSTM layer, and a hidden dimension of only 16 is a
deliberately lean model. The training data is a rolling window of recent creek
behavior, not a vast historical archive, and a larger model on that much data
would overfit, memorizing the recent past rather than learning the creek's
general rhythm. A small model also trains in seconds on a CPU, which matters
because the data is a moving window and we retrain the model regularly to keep
it current rather than training it once and freezing it. We sized the model to
the data that actually exists, and not to what a larger model would suggest on
paper.

\section{Data and Notation}
\label{sec:data}

We represent the creek as a graph with $N$ sensor nodes whose connections follow
the direction water flows. At each timestep every node carries a vector of
features: conductivity, depth, and temperature from the sensors, air
temperature, rainfall, and solar radiation merged in from weather data when it
is available, and four cyclical time encodings, i.e., the sine and cosine of
hour of day and of day of year. We did not hardcode the number of features. The
model reads the feature count from the data it is given at runtime and sizes its
input and output layers to match, which is why it adapts cleanly when a weather
channel is missing. We write $F$ for that count and treat $F = 10$ as the
deployed case throughout. Table~\ref{tab:notation} collects the symbols used in
the rest of this document.

A single training example is a window of $T = 24$ consecutive timesteps,

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:window}
  \mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times N \times F},
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{X}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times F}$ is the snapshot of every node's
features at timestep $t$. The target is the snapshot immediately after the
window:

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:target}
  \mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}_{T+1} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times F}.
\end{equation}

We chose $T = 24$ because it is six hours of history at the creek's fifteen
minute cadence. That is long enough to capture the daily rhythms the creek
actually has (e.g., the warming through the afternoon, the overnight runoff from
sprinklers) without reaching so far back that old conditions drown out the
recent trend the model is trying to extend.

Before anything else, we normalize every feature to a z-score using statistics
from complete data only:

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:zscore}
  z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}.
\end{equation}

This choice has a consequence that the model relies on. After normalization a value of
zero is the average, that is, the neutral middle of the distribution. That is
exactly the placeholder we use for missing sensor readings, so a gap contributes
nothing rather than dragging the model up or down. This is what lets the model
run on a network where some sites are offline: a sensor that is down simply
reads as the neutral zero everywhere it is missing.

\subsection{The deployed four-node core}
\label{sec:graph}

It is worth making the graph concrete for the network the model actually runs
on. The four active nodes, in the order the code indexes them, are north fork 0,
south fork 2, south fork 1, and oxford. We connect them with directed edges
following the physical direction of water flow downhill. There are three of
them:

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:edges}
  \text{north fork 0} \rightarrow \text{oxford}, \quad
  \text{south fork 1} \rightarrow \text{south fork 2}, \quad
  \text{south fork 2} \rightarrow \text{oxford}.
\end{equation}

Writing these as a directed adjacency $\mathbf{A}$, where entry $(i,j)$ is one
when there is an edge from node $i$ to node $j$, in the code's index order (north
fork 0, south fork 2, south fork 1, oxford) gives

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:adjacency}
  \mathbf{A} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\
    0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0
  \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

Reading the rows of Equation~\ref{eq:adjacency}: north fork 0 flows to oxford,
south fork 2 flows to oxford, south fork 1 flows to south fork 2, and oxford
flows nowhere, since it is the most downstream point. That last empty row is the
signature of a sink, i.e., the confluence where the network ends. The graph
convolution then adds a self loop to every node and applies the degree
normalization described in Section~\ref{sec:gcn}, so each node's update blends
its own value with the values flowing into it along these edges. The structure
is sparse and entirely determined by the creek's physical layout, which means
the model does not have to ``learn'' who connects to whom. We supply the
connectivity directly, and it is correct by construction.

\subsection{Why footbridge is not a node}
\label{sec:footbridge}

An earlier version of this network had five nodes, with footbridge sitting
between north fork 0 and oxford on the north branch. We removed footbridge and
contracted its edge, so north fork 0 now flows directly to oxford. The water
still runs that way; there is simply no sensor in between.

The reason is a mismatch between training and deployment. Inside the corpus
window footbridge has 1,191 real readings against 29,123 timesteps, i.e., 4.1
percent real coverage, and it does not appear in the live data cache at all.
Everything else was imputation. A node that is almost entirely fabricated in
training and entirely absent in production teaches the model to lean on a signal
that will not be there when it runs, and it fed that fabricated signal to its
graph neighbours as though it were real. Contracting the edge keeps the flow
topology honest and trains the model on the same set of sites it serves.

\section{Model Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

\subsection{The spatial step, a graph convolution}
\label{sec:gcn}

At each timestep we let each node's features be informed by the nodes connected
to it, because a contaminant upstream shows up downstream as the water carries
it. With $\mathbf{H} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times F}$ the node features at one
timestep, the graph convolution \citep{kipf2017gcn} computes:

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:gcn}
  \mathbf{H}' = \operatorname{ReLU}\!\left( \hat{\mathbf{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}} \, \hat{\mathbf{A}} \, \hat{\mathbf{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}} \, \mathbf{H} \, \mathbf{W} \right),
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{F \times d}$ is a learned weight matrix
projecting the $F$ features into the hidden size $d = 16$, and the nonlinearity
is

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:relu}
  \operatorname{ReLU}(x) = \max(0, x).
\end{equation}

This is the standard graph convolutional operator. The adjacency with inserted
self loops is $\hat{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{A} + \mathbf{I}$, and
$\hat{\mathbf{D}}$ is its diagonal degree matrix with $\hat{D}_{ii} = \sum_j
\hat{A}_{ij}$. The self loops let each node keep its own features rather than
only seeing neighbors, and the degree scaling $\hat{\mathbf{D}}^{-1/2}$ on both
sides keeps a heavily connected site from swamping a lightly connected one. The
graph library \citep{fey2019pyg} adds the self loops and applies this
normalization automatically, so the edge list we give the model does not need to
include them.

The matrix form in Equation~\ref{eq:gcn} is compact, but it hides what happens
to one site. Written per node, the new representation of node $i$ is a
normalized sum over itself and the nodes connected to it:

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:gcn-per-node}
  \mathbf{h}'_i = \operatorname{ReLU}\!\left( \sum_{j \,\in\, \mathcal{N}(i) \,\cup\, \{i\}} \frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{d}_i \, \hat{d}_j}} \, \mathbf{h}_j \, \mathbf{W} \right),
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{N}(i)$ is the set of nodes connected to $i$ through the graph and
$\hat{d}_i$ is node $i$'s degree including its self loop. The intuition is as
follows: each node's updated features are a degree-weighted blend of its own and
its neighbors', passed through the learned projection $\mathbf{W}$ and the
nonlinearity. This is what it means to borrow from your neighbors. A
spike in conductivity at a site therefore enters the connected site's
representation on the very next layer, which is exactly the propagation we need
to recognize a real event as it moves through the creek.

Two design choices live here. The first is the type of convolution: we set the
GCN type explicitly in the configuration, and a GCN weights neighbors by the
fixed graph structure, which is the right prior when the connections are real
water flow rather than relationships the model should infer. One way to do this
would be to let the model learn its own neighbor weightings instead. However,
the flow topology is known physics, so there is little reason to make the model
learn weightings it would only overfit on limited data. The second choice is
depth: we use a single graph layer, which means each node sees its immediate
neighbors. On a network this small, with short flow paths, one hop already
reaches the sites that matter, and stacking more layers would blur every node
toward a single averaged value, a known failure of deep graph networks called
oversmoothing \citep{li2018deeper}.

\subsection{Pooling, collapsing the network into one creek state}
\label{sec:pooling}

The temporal model wants a single vector summarising the whole creek at each
timestep alongside the per-node detail, so we average across the nodes:

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:pooling}
  \mathbf{h}_t = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{H}'_i \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{d}.
\end{equation}

Doing this at every timestep produces a sequence $\mathbf{h}_1, \dots,
\mathbf{h}_T$, each entry a summary of the whole connected network at one
moment. We use mean pooling rather than a more elaborate combination because it
is the simple, robust choice, and it interacts well with the missing-data
handling: when a node mask is supplied we take the average over the reporting
sites only, so the creek summary reflects real data rather than placeholder
zeros.

The pooled summary does not replace the per-node representations, it accompanies
them. An earlier version pooled and discarded $\mathbf{H}'$, which left the
temporal model with no way to tell one site from another and made every node
receive the same prediction. We carry both forward to the next step.

\subsection{The temporal step, an LSTM}
\label{sec:lstm}

The sequence goes into a single layer Long Short Term Memory network
\citep{hochreiter1997lstm}, which tracks how the signal evolves and carries
relevant information forward across steps. At each timestep it maintains a
hidden state $\mathbf{h}_t^{\text{lstm}}$ and a cell state $\mathbf{c}_t$,
updated through gates:

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:lstm}
  \begin{aligned}
    \mathbf{f}_t &= \sigma\!\left( \mathbf{W}_f \mathbf{h}_t + \mathbf{U}_f \mathbf{h}_{t-1}^{\text{lstm}} + \mathbf{b}_f \right), \\
    \mathbf{i}_t &= \sigma\!\left( \mathbf{W}_i \mathbf{h}_t + \mathbf{U}_i \mathbf{h}_{t-1}^{\text{lstm}} + \mathbf{b}_i \right), \\
    \mathbf{o}_t &= \sigma\!\left( \mathbf{W}_o \mathbf{h}_t + \mathbf{U}_o \mathbf{h}_{t-1}^{\text{lstm}} + \mathbf{b}_o \right), \\
    \tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t &= \tanh\!\left( \mathbf{W}_c \mathbf{h}_t + \mathbf{U}_c \mathbf{h}_{t-1}^{\text{lstm}} + \mathbf{b}_c \right), \\
    \mathbf{c}_t &= \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{c}_{t-1} + \mathbf{i}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t, \\
    \mathbf{h}_t^{\text{lstm}} &= \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tanh(\mathbf{c}_t).
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The three gates are each squashed by the sigmoid $\sigma$ into values between
zero and one, acting as soft switches. The symbol $\odot$ is elementwise
multiplication, and the $\mathbf{W}$, $\mathbf{U}$, and $\mathbf{b}$ are
learned. The intuition behind this network is that the forget gate
$\mathbf{f}_t$ decides how much of the previous memory to keep, the input gate
$\mathbf{i}_t$ how much of the new candidate $\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t$ to store, and
the output gate $\mathbf{o}_t$ how much of the resulting memory to emit. The
cell update line is where the network actually remembers or forgets.

We chose the LSTM over a plain recurrent network because its gates let it hold a
trend from hours earlier when relevant and discard it when not, which matters
for a six hour window. We use a single LSTM layer for the same reason the rest
of the model is small: the sequences are short and the data is limited, so one
layer captures the temporal pattern without overfitting. We keep the final
hidden state,

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:final-state}
  \mathbf{h}_T^{\text{lstm}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d},
\end{equation}

which is the model's compressed ``understanding'' of how the creek has been
behaving over the whole window.

\subsection{The output, predicting the next reading}
\label{sec:output}

The temporal model runs per node rather than once for the whole creek. For node
$i$ at timestep $t$ the input is that node's own graph convolution output, the
pooled creek summary, and a learned embedding of the node's identity,
concatenated:

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:lstm-input}
  \mathbf{z}_{i,t} = \left[\, \mathbf{H}'_{i,t} \;\Vert\; \mathbf{h}_t \;\Vert\; \mathbf{e}_i \,\right] \in \mathbb{R}^{2d + d_e},
\end{equation}

with $\mathbf{e}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d_e}$, $d_e = 8$, learned one vector per site.
The node embedding is what lets the model ``know'' that south fork 2 normally
runs hotter than north fork 0 rather than treating every site as
interchangeable. The sequence $\mathbf{z}_{i,1}, \dots, \mathbf{z}_{i,T}$ goes
through the LSTM of Equation~\ref{eq:lstm}, and the final hidden state
$\mathbf{h}_{i,T}^{\text{lstm}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ is that node's own compressed
history.

The output layer predicts a change from the last observed reading rather than an
absolute value:

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:output}
  \hat{\mathbf{Y}}_i = \mathbf{X}_{T,i} + \left( \mathbf{W}_{\text{out}} \, \mathbf{h}_{i,T}^{\text{lstm}} + \mathbf{b}_{\text{out}} \right),
\end{equation}

with $\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}} \in \mathbb{R}^{F \times d}$ and
$\mathbf{b}_{\text{out}} \in \mathbb{R}^{F}$ learned, and $\mathbf{X}_{T,i}$ the
node's features at the last timestep of the window, feature-propagated if a mask
filled it in. Everything is in normalized units, so no denormalization happens
here.

The residual form in Equation~\ref{eq:output} is the reason the model has any
notion of where things currently are. One way to build the output would be to
predict from the hidden state alone. However, the model then falls back on
whatever average shape minimizes error across the training set, which is
climatology rather than a forecast.

Both choices show up in the predictions. On the deployed checkpoint, the spread
across nodes, that is, the largest node prediction minus the smallest, reaches
4.592 in normalized units. Decomposing it, the learned head contributes 0.188
and the residual anchor contributes 4.591. Concretely, almost all of the
per-node difference comes from each node starting at its own last reading, and
the head supplies a comparatively small correction on top. A version without
node identity would give every node the same number, i.e., a spread of exactly
zero.

\section{Training}
\label{sec:training}

We train by minimizing the mean squared error between prediction and truth,

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:loss}
  \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{\lvert \mathcal{M} \rvert \, \lvert \mathcal{S} \rvert} \sum_{(i) \in \mathcal{M}} \; \sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}} \left( \hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{ij} - \mathbf{Y}_{ij} \right)^2,
\end{equation}

over training data that contains only ordinary creek behavior. The model learns
the absence of spills, not spills themselves.

Two restrictions narrow that sum. $\mathcal{S}$ is the scored channel set, that
is, conductivity, depth and temperature, not all $F$ channels. The model still
predicts every channel it was given. However, rainfall 15 minutes ahead is not
learnable from creek sensors, and scoring it adds irreducible error that is
worst during storms, which is exactly when conductivity matters most.
$\mathcal{M}$ is the set of node targets that were actually observed and passed
quality control. A target that was never read is filled with the neutral zero,
and scoring the model against that fabricated zero teaches it to reproduce an
absence.

Two further training choices are worth naming. We apply dropout of 0.1 during
training, randomly zeroing a small fraction of hidden units each step, which
prevents the small model from leaning too hard on any single pattern. Training
runs for up to 10 epochs with early stopping at patience 5. That is, if the
validation loss stops improving for five epochs the run halts and the best
weights are restored, so the model does not train past the point where it is
still learning something general. We train in plain 32 bit precision on CPU,
because the mixed precision that speeds up large models on a graphics card
actually breaks the LSTM computation on a CPU and offers no benefit at this
size.

\section{Detection}
\label{sec:detection}

\subsection{Model all, alert one}
\label{sec:score}

Once trained, the model runs on new data and we examine where it missed. The
prediction error at node $i$ on feature $j$ is the absolute deviation:

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:error}
  e_{ij} = \left\lvert \hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{ij} - \mathbf{Y}_{ij} \right\rvert.
\end{equation}

The defining choice is which of those errors we act on. The model predicts all
features, but we use only conductivity error to decide whether something is
anomalous. This is the model all, alert one strategy. Conductivity is the
channel that actually responds to spills, while the others, rainfall especially,
produce large prediction errors that have nothing to do with water quality. An
earlier version that scored on the average error across all features was
effectively flagging rainstorms, because the rain prediction error swamped
everything. Isolating conductivity removed those false alarms.

We keep the score per node rather than averaging it across the network. Writing
$c$ for the conductivity index, node $i$'s score at a timestep is $e_{ic}$, and
we judge it against that node's own history rather than a shared bar:

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:score}
  s_i = \frac{e_{ic} - \operatorname{median}_i}{\operatorname{IQR}_i},
\end{equation}

where $\operatorname{median}_i$ and $\operatorname{IQR}_i$ are the median and
interquartile range of node $i$'s conductivity error over held out validation
data. Averaging across nodes was the earlier design, on the reasoning that an
event propagating through the network should score higher than a single site
blip. We replaced it because sites differ: one that simply runs noisier than the
rest sits permanently above a shared threshold, and averaging lets a large
deviation at one node be diluted by three quiet ones. Robust normalization per
node fixes both, and keeping the node axis is what lets an alert say which site
is affected.

The threshold is also per node, fixed once during training as the 99th
percentile of that node's normalized validation errors:

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:threshold}
  \tau_i = \operatorname{percentile}_{99}\big( \{ s_i^{\text{val}} \} \big).
\end{equation}

We fix it at training time rather than recomputing it on whatever data is in
front of the model, and that is deliberate: recomputing would be circular and
would let the threshold drift with each batch. The 99th percentile means that
under normal conditions only about one percent of timesteps at a node would
cross it, which sets the false alarm rate before deployment rather than
discovering it after. A single timestep over the bar is not an alert. A node is
flagged only when at least three scored timesteps in the window cross it, so one
noisy reading cannot raise an alarm.

We compute median, interquartile range and threshold on observed targets only. A
node target that was never read is filled with the neutral zero, and letting
those into the calibration sets the bar against data that does not exist. This
matters in practice: on the current corpus one site had 27.7 percent of its
target cells fabricated, and its threshold was calibrated almost entirely
against the error of predicting a zero.

\subsection{The second rule, sustained level shift}
\label{sec:rule2}

The error based rule above is structurally blind to one shape. A clean step in
conductivity ``surprises'' the model once and is then absorbed into its own
forecast, so the residual goes quiet while the water stays abnormal. A second
rule watches the level itself rather than the error:

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:level-shift}
  u_i = \frac{\mathbf{Y}_{ic} - \operatorname{median}^{\text{lvl}}_i}{\operatorname{IQR}^{\text{lvl}}_i}, \qquad \lvert u_i \rvert > k,
\end{equation}

with $k = 4.0$ and the median and interquartile range taken over the node's
normalized conductivity level on validation data. A node is anomalous if either
rule fires, and the result records which one did, since an onset and a sustained
abnormal level mean different things operationally.

\subsection{The rain adjustment}
\label{sec:rain}

Rain genuinely raises conductivity without being a spill, as runoff carries road
salt and grime into the creek. To avoid alarming on ordinary rain, we raise the
threshold during wet periods. Summing rainfall over the preceding 12 hour
window, the per timestep threshold is

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:rain}
  \tau_t =
  \begin{cases}
    m \cdot \tau & \text{if } \sum_{k \in \text{window}} r_k > \theta, \\[4pt]
    \tau \left( 1 + (m - 1)\left(1 - \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{\Delta_t}{D}, 0, 1\right)\right) \right) & \text{otherwise, if it rained within } D \text{ hours},\\[4pt]
    \tau & \text{otherwise,}
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $r_k$ is the rainfall at timestep $k$, $\theta = 0.1$ the small amount that
counts as wet, $m = 2.0$ the multiplier, $D = 36$ hours the post rain decay
window, and $\Delta_t$ the hours elapsed since the lookback window last closed on
wet weather. That is, we double the bar while rain is recent, and then ease it
back to normal over a day and a half rather than dropping it in one step. The 12
hour window and the doubling are the tuned values. They suppress the routine
first flush of a storm while still leaving room to catch a deviation large
enough to clear twice the normal bar.

The taper exists because the step alone had a gap. With a hard edge the
threshold returns to normal the moment the lookback window clears, so a delayed
first flush pulse arriving a few hours later meets the ordinary bar and is
flagged as a spill. The decay covers that window and returns smoothly instead.

There is a limit in the other direction. A genuine spill that happens to occur
during or shortly after rain has to clear a bar that is up to twice as high, so
real events in wet weather are harder to detect, and one confirmed event in the
catalog is missed for exactly this reason. Where to set $m$ and $D$ is a trade
between missing spills in the wet and alarming on ordinary runoff, and it is a
judgment about what the system should alarm on rather than a flaw in the math.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

Nearly every choice in the design points the same
direction: keep the model small and matched to limited, rolling data, encode the
known physics of water flow directly into the graph rather than making the model
learn it, and decide anomalies on the one channel that actually carries the
signal.

Three of the limitations named in an earlier version of this document have since
been addressed, and it is worth saying which. We added cyclical time features,
so the model now has hour of day and day of year context and no longer reads an
out of season window as unusual everywhere. The loss scores conductivity, depth
and temperature rather than conductivity alone. And we widened the post rain
window into the decay described in Section~\ref{sec:rain}. Those were listed as
natural next steps and we took them.

What remains is a shorter list. The first two are the most important.

Scoring on conductivity alone means an anomaly that shows up only in another
channel (e.g., a dissolved oxygen crash from an oil sheen) would not be caught
by the current alarm. The loss now watches three channels, but detection still
watches one.

The rain adjustment gives up some detection in order to suppress false alarms.
A spill during or shortly after
rain has to clear up to twice the ordinary bar, and at least one confirmed event
in the catalog is not detected under the deployed multiplier. Lowering it would
recover that event at the cost of alarming on ordinary runoff. We chose these
values by judgment rather than deriving them, and they belong to whoever owns
the alerting budget.

The network is four nodes, not five. We dropped footbridge for the coverage
reason given in Section~\ref{sec:footbridge}, which keeps training and
deployment consistent but leaves the north branch with no sensor between north
fork 0 and oxford. An event entering there is seen only once it reaches oxford.

The threshold rests on a single chronological split of a corpus that begins in
June 2025. The held out portion covers a season the training portion does not
contain, so distribution shift and overfitting cannot be fully separated with the
data as it stands. We leave a same season holdout, which would separate them, as
an open question for future work.

Finally, we retrain the model on a rolling window rather than training it once
and freezing it, so every number in this document describes the current
checkpoint rather than the architecture in perpetuity. The thresholds in
particular are fitted quantities and will move when the model is refitted.
